translating legal documents with ai

a service, not a product. on purpose.

running now · 20 documents · 4 lawyers · paid per page

it found me

i wasn't looking for this one.

i was at a friend's place. his brother is a lawyer, and he knew i spend most of my time messing around with claude and codex, so he asked whether ai could translate hindi legal documents into english. i said probably. opus 4.8 was the newest model at the time and it was handling harder things than that.

it was a saturday. he had a hearing on monday. the human translators had told him thursday at the earliest, and his only other options were paying them more to rush it and pushing the hearing futher.

so i asked him what would break it.

no copying signatures or stamps.

the meaning cannot shift.

formatting and alignment have to match the original.

no invented jargon.

and the words have to be literal. if someone has passed away, the document says he is dead. if a woman is expecting, the document says she's pregnant. no softening it.

that list turned out to be the entire spec.

what i actually built

i gave claude the full brief and asked it to write its own prompt back to me: work out which tools and skills the job needs, translate, then output a pdf with the formatting and alignment intact.

then, one by one, i fed it 5 documents. sixty to seventy pages in total.

after every batch i asked his brother to read it before finalising. whatever he flagged went straight back into the system. it got measurably better after each document, because someone who actually knew the law was checking every page.

he was surprised by the accuracy. then he paid me, which i hadn't asked for, because i'd saved him a week and the potential rush fee.

he also told me the real problem isn't translation. it's that human translators are slow and moody and miss deadlines, and the whole case timeline bends around them.

then it spread without me

my uncle is a lawyer too, same complaint about translators. he was reluctant at first. i did one document, he read it, and asked me to take most of them.

he's the one who called it more than 95% accurate. he also said that if i turned it into a product, we wouldn't go to human translators at all.

he referred his lawyer friends. they pay me too. i do the first two or three documents for free so people can see it work, and after that it's per page.

twenty documents in, four lawyers currently. i have done nothing to find any of them. all of it has been word of mouth, and that's the number i pay most attention to.

the hard part

getting from 95% to 99%.

i assumed volume would do it. it doesn't. accuracy plateaus.

what i do now is ask the system to write itself a stronger prompt every five or six documents, and log every new finding in a claude.md file. that helps. it isn't the answer.

the answer is that it needs real legal knowledge: the terms, the jargon, what a judge is reading for, what a filing is supposed to sound like. and you can't do that inside a chat session, because the context keeps growing and response quality falls off as it does.

which means it has to become a product built for this one job.

i don't know how to do that yet. i'm researching it, asking people on reddit, and asking the lawyers themselves what the system needs to know. that's honestly where it stands.

why i'm running it by hand

everything stride taught me the expensive way, i'm doing deliberately this time.

stride started from a question i found interesting. this started from someone's actual monday deadline.

with stride i built for months and talked to users late. here the lawyers read every page before it goes out, and their corrections go into the system the same day.

with stride we deferred revenue until the savings ran out. this made money on the first document.

and with stride we kept adding features because building was fun. so i'm not building the product yet. the system isn't good enough to productise, and shipping a tool that's 95% right on a court filing is worse than shipping nothing at all.

i'm also asking every lawyer what else they'd pay an ai to do. some of those answers are more interesting than translation.

i'm not betting my life on this one. it pays, it teaches me something every week, and it might turn into something big. mostly it taught me that when you find a problem someone is already paying badly to solve, you don't have to convince anyone of anything.

… refining as i learn
